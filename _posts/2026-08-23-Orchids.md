The other day I was sent a lovely present: a vase flush with flowers. The audacious sunflower was the protagonist, of course, but it was supported by the orange roses on one side and a spray of purple flowers on the other, and set off nicely by the constellations of little white blooms that encircled them all. 

I learned that the purple flowers were orchids, and the white ones were feverfews; also that saying that a flower is an “orchid” is like saying a plant is a “tree”. There are nearly twenty thousand species, but they can be easily distinguished from other flowers by looking at the petals. They will appear to have six; actually, the three on the outside are really the sepals that once enclosed the bud. And of the three petals on the inside, the true petals,  two will look alike, but one of them will look a little like a lip — and in fact, is called exactly that (or a *labellum,* to be Latinate about it). It is a landing pad into the heart of the flower, purpose-built for the bee or the wasp that will hopefully pollinate it. 

What was the point of learning all this? Whether I see purple flowers, or *orchids*, or even *Dendrobium bigibbum —* whether the special petal is a landing pad for insects, or the bottom lip of a mischievous girl who transgressed against a vengeful god and was transformed into a flower — the purple flowers remain purple flowers, just as beautiful to me as to an unlettered child, or to a bee for that matter. I am not a botanist; being able to tell orchids apart from other flowers is of no practical use to me. So what explains my pleasure in this newfound knowledge?

Surely it is that my view of the world has been *enriched*, made more complex. What was an anonymous gape of flora has now been dignified with a name and made intelligible as part of the works of nature. It has acquired a botanical dimension. I could look into the history of the cultivation of this particular flower, or what it has signified in various cultures, and it would acquire new dimensions — historical, cultural — that would be overlaid on the original one, the flower as simple aesthetic experience. 

This process that makes everything in the world, whether flower, poem, star, or thought itself, more intelligible and more complex — it goes by the modest name of “education”.
