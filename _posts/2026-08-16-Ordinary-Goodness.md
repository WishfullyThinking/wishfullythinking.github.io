For all that characters can be divided into the “good guys” and the “bad guys”, I find very few convincing depictions of good people in art. By good I don’t mean heroic — that’s a totally different concept. I mean good in the sense of _prosocial_ — someone who is considerate and altruistic on a human scale. Perhaps I had better call such a person “ordinarily good”. The ordinarily good person comforts lost children, gives up their bus seat to the infirm and tired, strikes up conversations with the lonely at lunch. They may not run NGOs for starving children, campaign for safer and more effective public transport, or further the cause of public mental health. In fact, I suspect that all of these require a certain toughness of mind that might hinder or preclude ordinary goodness. Florence Nightingale bullied and terrified her allies and enemies alike, but she would not have been the humanitarian force that she was if she couldn’t arm-twist politicians into doing her bidding. At these levels, I feel almost as though it’s qualitatively different from ordinary goodness, and more akin to a style of life, like that of an artist or a hermit. Nevertheless, I also suspect that the role of extraordinary goodness is to let human beings be ordinarily good to each other. 

Sound, Euphonium is a remarkable show because of how strong a sense it has for ordinary goodness. Being set in a high school, where the stakes are necessarily less than mortal, actually helps it in this regard. The contest between Kaori and Reina for the trumpet solo is really the clash between the ordinarily good and the extraordinarily talented. If Reina were ordinarily good, she might have voluntarily given up the part to Kaori. Kaori is a highly competent performer who was denied the solo part for two years running, only because it was then handed out by seniority rather than merit. Kaori is kind; Kaori is selfless; everyone loves Kaori and would hate to see her sad. But Reina has no desire to please or comfort others. She is dedicated to beautiful music and outraged that others might instead prefer a banal harmony. That the trumpet solo is hers by right is the brute fact that she forces all her bandmates to admit to, by sheer dint of talent and belief. She is bewitching to Kumiko, and to me, too; but the spell has nothing to do with her goodness. She remains, from beginning to end, proud, unsociable, and exacting; she is extraordinary and unabashed about it. 

Meanwhile, Kaori is ordinarily good to an extraordinary degree. She is all decency and kindness, even when she suffers for it; she is socially intelligent enough to know that her rival will be ostracised, and in inviting Reina to have lunch with the rest, generous enough to prevent it from happening even through inaction. It is this last detail that makes Kaori so convincingly good. Till the point was raised, I hadn’t even thought about it, but Kaori did.  It marks Kaori out among the rest of the cast, who are either mundanely selfish, or at best partial to their friends. It puts Reina to shame, too.

The humane Kaori concedes defeat, of course. There is nothing fair about the division of talent. No doubt it would suit our sentiments more if Kaori were the prodigy instead — but would we want that? If Kaori were the younger prodigy, and Reina the senior, Kaori would likely have surrendered without a fight — and done her talent the injustice that the real Reina never would. Reina’s coarseness and brutality  protect her talent, even as they introduce friction between herself and others that has to be resolved, typically by Kumiko. The only consolation for Kaori — if consolation it is — is that Reina’s gift is a brilliant flame that dazzles and amazes, but cannot be shared with another. Kaori’s ordinary goodness is not a “gift”. It may have begun as a set of mental dispositions towards pleasing others, but she has thoughtfully cultivated them and risen beyond mere inoffensiveness into a force for social cohesion. We can learn from her, as we can’t from Reina. Kumiko certainly does, consciously or not, and in doing so becomes a great leader — even if just of a school wind ensemble. For everyday life, that is more than sufficient.
