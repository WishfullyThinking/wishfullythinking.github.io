 In their inimitably one-eyed way, the economists think that people act exactly as they desire — or close enough, anyway, for their models to hold water. Rose Cipollone died of lung cancer in 1984. Lay a Chesterfield cigarette, a Liggett and Meyer, a Parliament, and a Lorillard’s True end-to-end, and you have measured out her life from age sixteen to fifty-eight. Economics would speak of her addiction as a “revealed preference”, or perhaps admit that smoking exacted an economic toll on society in terms of the expenses of her healthcare as well as the opportunity cost of her labour. But that is not the decent thing to say. The decent thing to say is that she 1) made an unfortunate decision at a young age, in a time when the ills of smoking were not well known and 2) failed to quit despite her best efforts, except to give up Chesterfields for Liggetts and Liggetts for Parliaments. She regretted her choice, just as we regret many of our own; and she failed to act on her regret, because of the nature of addiction — and of tobacco marketing. 

One regrets a past decision in respect of a different one that was feasible at the time and — it is imagined — would have led one to outcomes that are pleasanter to live with. It is all too easy to imagine this with cigarettes (what is not so easy is the question of how feasible quitting was). But it can be far more difficult to perform such an analysis for other decisions. One can regret being “terminally online” in a low moment, in favour of productive activities, but on reflection consider that, after all, it has led one to fulfilling, enduring friendships. It is also possible for both — or neither — of two decisions to lead to regrets down the line. If the choice was between being a lawyer or a comedian, perhaps a lucky chap could be crowned with success and satisfaction in both fields, whatever he chose. Or perhaps, more interestingly, he might discover a fascination with the study of the law that makes the question of success much less relevant: he is then a different person, in terms of his preferences and valuations. Things become more complicated if we add a third choice of profession: a con artist scamming the elderly for a living. If our man becomes the kind of person to relish it, is that choice — absent any pangs of conscience — “regrettable” at all? Not, perhaps, from his perspective as a con artist. However, the emotionally fulfilled and professionally successful lawyer could laugh at himself for having once upon a time seriously considered a life of crime, and from the bottom of his heart thank the heavens that he chose right. This is the exact inverse of regret — we don’t have a word for this kind of self-satisfaction, so perhaps we had better call it anti-regret. One can feel anti-regret in clear-cut cases, like not procrastinating on important work. And one can feel it in cases where more of an act of imagination is necessary, as with our lawyer friend. 

Regret (and anti-regret) have both intrinsic and instrumental value; in fact, the intrinsic and the instrumental are conjoined. The injunction don’t do things you’ll regret later recognises that our regret for a poor decision is often more painful than the harm it has caused us: “it is perhaps itself a punishment adequate to the crime by which it was produced”, in Dr Johnson’s phrase. The possibility of regret makes us choose and act more carefully; the consciousness of regret induces us to do better next time. Actionable regret is then one of our noblest emotions. Conversely, when regret is not actionable beyond a token gesture, one can accept the pain as a permanent censure — “make peace” with it — or attempt to ignore it. (This is the fate of Stevens in Remains of the Day.) The worst kind of regret, however, is that kind which is actionable, but that we fail to act on. Because regret is so exquisitely painful, it may be expedient to simply lower our standards so that we do not incur any further regret. The choice was not a choice after all — constituted as I am, I could not have acted otherwise. And who knows whether the “right choice” would have led me to greater happiness? The inner machinations that contrive to minimise regret — in the crude modern parlance, cope — change us in ways that are as difficult to quantify as they are real in their effect. The lack of regret of the con artist can and should be suspected on these grounds. 

If one of the goals of the good life is to make our way out of it with our souls more or less intact, we must consider technologies from this psychological angle.
