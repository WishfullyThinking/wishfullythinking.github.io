I’ve come to think that the “art makes people more moral/empathetic” argument is an attempt to invest art with Utility. It’s not a particularly modern position — Samuel Johnson was arguing that realistic novels should be tools for moral instruction as recently as 1750 — but I think it fundamentally misunderstands what art does to us. 

It’s a bit like saying, *coffee is good because it helps us stay alert*. Yes, that is technically true, and many people do drink it for exactly that reason. But those people don’t really like coffee *for itself*. Any anti-soporific would do as well, coffee just happens to be cheap and legal. People who *do* like coffee, however, like it because of how it tastes and feels, and those who *really* like coffee seek out all the subtle variations in it to see how it affects them. Art is far more complicated than coffee because the mental levers are much more various than smell and taste. But the effect is similar: it awakens emotions and sentiments we didn’t know we had, or didn’t know could be experienced in conjunction. It doesn’t make us *morally better* — we are better off volunteering or doing social work if that’s the goal. But it does make us more aware of the subtlety and complications that lurk within us but we aren’t aware of.

I feel like part of the reason the art-as-empathy-generator theory is tempting and pervasive is that ethics and morality are unquestionably *also* aesthetic criteria, especially in literature. If someone failed to grasp, for instance, that Humbert is evil — or that Henry and Mary Crawford are, in all their brilliance, appalling — they would fail to grasp *Lolita* or *Mansfield Park*, just like how the best coffee would be wasted on someone with a bad cold. The empathy generator theory gets things the wrong way around: it posits that aesthetics serves ethics rather than vice versa. But vice versa it is.

There’s a sort of devaluation of aesthetics, I think, in preference to ethics, that has taken hold in modern life? I think we ask that what used to go by the name of “cultivation” justify itself, produce something of Objective Value. When the result of art is really to please us (at the ground level) and to complicate us (very rarely). The first can at least be quantified via box office gross or sales figures, but the second is elusive. But it *does* matter.
